\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

The pheromone-field coordination of Section~\ref{sec:method} is evaluated on meeting room scheduling, assigning meetings to rooms over a five-day horizon to remove unscheduled meetings and attendee overlaps. The domain supplies a sensed pressure field with measurable success and difficulty that scales with problem size, while staying small enough that hierarchical and conversational baselines remain feasible to run. The question is where decentralized symmetric-stigmergic coordination outperforms hierarchical control, and where it does not.

\textbf{Reporting note.} An earlier draft reported parity between pheromone-field and hierarchical control on a smaller pilot. The full grid then showed a regime-dependent gap, and a code audit found two defects in the measurement path: a system-prompt confound that advantaged some baselines under a Latin-square assignment, and a pressure-metric mismatch across strategies. Both are fixed, the strategies now share one authoritative pressure metric, and every result below comes from the regenerated 1350-trial grid.

\subsection{Setup}

\subsubsection{Task: Meeting Room Scheduling}

We generate scheduling problems with varying difficulty:

\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}

\textbf{Difficulty} & \textbf{Rooms} & \textbf{Meetings} & \textbf{Pre-scheduled} \\
\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Problem configurations. Pre-scheduled percentage indicates meetings already placed; remaining meetings must be scheduled by agents.}
\label{tab:problems}
\end{table}

Each schedule spans 5 days with 30-minute time slots (8am--4pm). Regions are 2-hour time blocks (4 blocks per day $\times$ 5 days = 20 regions per schedule). A problem is ``solved'' when all meetings are scheduled with zero attendee overlaps within 50 ticks.

\textbf{One authoritative pressure metric.} All reported pressure, for every strategy, is $P = 1.0 \cdot (\text{unscheduled meetings}) + 10.0 \cdot (\text{attendee overlaps})$ on the schedule grid, so cross-strategy comparisons measure coordination rather than metric choice. The sensor signals that feed activation (gap ratio, overlap count, utilization variance per block) are kept separate from this reported metric: they drive only the activation field and the hierarchical baseline's heuristic, never the numbers in the tables below.

\textbf{Alignment verification.} Two facts secure alignment in this domain. First, the per-region components are strictly local to each time block, and patches rewrite a single block, so modifying region $i$ leaves region $j$'s pressure unchanged ($\epsilon = 0$); the one cross-region channel is the unscheduled pool, whose cost enters the total metric directly, attributed to no region. Second, the acceptance gate evaluates the full metric: a patch is applied only if $P$, including the unscheduled term, strictly decreases, so alignment holds by construction even for patches that displace meetings into the pool. Appendix~\ref{app:coupling} reports the empirical check.

\subsubsection{Baselines}

We compare five coordination strategies, all using identical \acp{llm} (\texttt{qwen2.5:0.5b/1.5b/3b} via Ollama) to isolate coordination effects:

\textbf{Pheromone-field (ours)}: Full system with pheromone evaporation (example bank $0.95$/tick, rejected-patch store), inhibition (2 logical ticks), one-hop ESP propagation ($\kappa = 0.5$), and two-stage validation. Includes band escalation (Exploitation $\to$ Balanced $\to$ Exploration, every 7 stalled ticks) and model escalation (0.5b $\to$ 1.5b $\to$ 3b, every 21 stalled ticks).

\textbf{Conversation}: AutoGen-style multi-agent dialogue where agents exchange messages to coordinate scheduling decisions. Agents discuss conflicts and propose solutions through explicit communication.

\textbf{Hierarchical}: Single agent selects the highest-pressure time block each tick, proposes a schedule change, and validates before applying (only accepts pressure-reducing patches). Uses the identical base prompt to pheromone-field; pheromone-field prompts additionally carry the few-shot examples and avoid-tips injected from the two pheromone stores. The differences are: (1) greedy region selection always targets the hardest region, and (2) sequential execution processes one region per tick. This represents centralized, quality-gated control.

\textbf{Escalation across strategies.} Every strategy, baselines included, receives model escalation on the same trigger, 21 stalled ticks stepping 0.5b $\to$ 1.5b $\to$ 3b, so no strategy is capability-starved relative to another. Band escalation is part of the pheromone-field mechanism only; the conversation baseline uses fixed sampling (T=0.3, top-p=0.9) suited to structured dialogue. The comparison therefore does not withhold capability from the baselines: what differs is whether escalated capability arrives at field-selected regions or at the single region a greedy controller keeps choosing.

\textbf{Sequential}: Single agent iterates through time blocks in fixed order, proposing schedule changes one region at a time. No parallelism, pressure guidance, or patch validation---applies any syntactically valid patch regardless of quality impact.

\textbf{Random}: Selects random time blocks and proposes schedule changes. No patch validation---applies any syntactically valid patch regardless of quality impact.

\textbf{Note on concurrency}: Pheromone-field addresses several distinct high-pressure regions in the same tick, each proposal validated against the shared artifact by the two-stage gate; hierarchical addresses one region per tick. This asymmetry follows from the coordination paradigm, not from an implementation choice: hierarchical control selects a region, delegates, and validates before proceeding, and dispatching to several regions at once would require the work-distribution, conflict-resolution, and aggregation protocols that define a different architecture. When hierarchical's single patch is rejected, the tick makes no progress; when one of pheromone-field's concurrent patches is rejected, the others can still apply.

\textbf{Model choice rationale}: We deliberately use small models (0.5b--3b parameters) rather than frontier models, identically across all strategies, to isolate coordination effects from model capability. A mechanism that extracts usable performance from weak models is valuable independent of model scale. Frontier models would plausibly raise absolute solve rates across all strategies; whether they preserve the relative ranking is an empirical question this study does not settle.

\subsubsection{Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Solve rate}: Percentage of schedules reaching all meetings placed with zero overlaps within 50 ticks.
\item \textbf{Ticks to solve}: Convergence speed for solved cases
\item \textbf{Final pressure}: Remaining unscheduled meetings and attendee overlaps for unsolved cases, on the authoritative metric
\item \textbf{Token efficiency}: Total prompt and completion tokens consumed per trial and per successful solve
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Implementation}

\textbf{Software}: Rust implementation with Ollama for local inference. \textbf{Grid}: 5 strategies $\times$ \{1, 2, 4\} agents $\times$ \{easy, medium, hard\} $\times$ 30 trials = 1350 trials, all with a 50-tick budget. \textbf{Determinism}: all temporal dynamics run on the runtime's logical tick clock, so results are deterministic and hardware-independent; each trial's problem is generated from a seed of $\text{trial} \times 1000 + \text{agent\_count}$, so a given trial faces an identical problem across all strategies. The ablation study is a separate 9-arm matrix (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}). Escalation parameters are specified in Section~\ref{sec:escalation}; the full protocol is in Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}

The ablation runs on medium problems, not easy: at the easy solve rate the base mechanism already succeeds on most instances, leaving no room to measure what each component adds. Medium leaves the full system at a $40\%$ solve rate, with headroom in both directions.

No single removal moves the solve rate significantly: every arm's Fisher exact $p$ against the full system exceeds $0.19$, and the all-off baseline ($36.7\%$) is statistically indistinguishable from the full system ($40.0\%$, $p = 1.0$). The large advantage of the full system over the conversational and hierarchical baselines (Section~\ref{sec:main-results}) therefore does not come from the four pheromone enhancements. It is structural: field-based concurrent dispatch addresses many high-pressure regions per tick, and the two-stage validation gate admits only strictly improving patches. Those two mechanisms are present in every arm of this matrix, the all-off baseline included, which is why removing the enhancements on top of them changes so little.

Pooling across the design sharpens the same reading. Each feature's marginal effect, computed as the solve rate with it enabled minus the rate with it disabled across all nine arms (roughly $135$ trials per side), is small and non-significant: evaporation $-0.8$ points ($p = 0.90$), inhibition $+5.2$ ($p = 0.45$), propagation $+4.8$ ($p = 0.55$), examples $-5.3$ ($p = 0.39$) (Figure~\ref{fig:contributions}). At $30$ trials per arm the design has power to detect only large effects; contributions of this size would need several hundred trials per arm to separate from zero, so the honest statement is that no temporal-dynamics component is individually decisive at this scale, not that each makes a precisely measured contribution.

The one consistent signal is that the example bank does not pay for itself. Removing it raises the solve rate to $60\%$, the best of any arm, and roughly halves token cost, because every prompt otherwise carries the few-shot examples; the deposited examples appear to mislead the small models more often than they help, a positive-pheromone analogue of a stale trail. The propagation arm, which isolates the $ESP$ operator added in this revision, shows a small positive direction ($+4.8$ points pooled) that does not reach significance; as a single-hop operator (Section~\ref{sec:symmetric-stigmergy}) it is a deliberately conservative instantiation, and multi-hop diffusion (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) is where a larger effect would be expected. Component removals leave time-to-solution unchanged as well: among solved trials, mean ticks differ by less than one across the evaporation, inhibition, and propagation contrasts.

\subsubsection{Negative Pheromones}

Alongside the positive example bank, a second pheromone store records harmful rejected patches as negative pheromones. On rejection of a harmful patch, the system deposits it on the artifact and injects it into later prompts for the same region. The injected text is phrased as positive guidance (``TIP: schedule meetings in Room A'') rather than a bare prohibition, restating what to try instead of what to avoid. This store evaporates on its own schedule, weight $\times 0.95$ per tick and evicted below 0.1, so stale avoid-tips lapse rather than permanently blocking a now-viable approach; up to three recent rejections per region are surfaced. The store carries no reinforcement on reuse, only deposit and evaporation.

\subsection{Band and Model Escalation}
\label{sec:escalation}

Escalation is the $ASU$ operator of Section~\ref{sec:symmetric-stigmergy} in operation: when the sensed field stops improving, the environment reallocates agent capability. It runs at two levels, both keyed to pressure stagnation (no improvement above 0.01) on the logical tick clock.

\emph{Band escalation} adjusts sampling within a model. After 7 stalled ticks the sampling band steps from Exploitation through Balanced to Exploration, widening the proposal distribution as progress stalls.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Band} & \textbf{Temperature} & \textbf{Top-p} \\
\midrule
Exploitation & 0.15--0.35 & 0.80--0.90 \\
Balanced & 0.35--0.55 & 0.85--0.95 \\
Exploration & 0.55--0.85 & 0.90--0.98 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Sampling parameter ranges per band; temperature and top-p are sampled within range. Escalation proceeds Exploitation $\to$ Balanced $\to$ Exploration every 7 stalled ticks.}
\label{tab:bands}
\end{table}

\emph{Model escalation} adjusts capability across the model chain. After 21 stalled ticks the model steps 0.5b $\to$ 1.5b $\to$ 3b, and the band resets to Exploitation so the larger model first exploits before widening its search. The escalation criterion is the pressure signal alone: a stalled field, not an explicit difficulty estimate, triggers the recruitment of more capable reasoning. The grid runs with escalation enabled throughout, and the $ASU$ arrow's contribution is visible in which model produces each solve: the share of solved trials that required stepping past the smallest model rises with difficulty, from $27\%$ on easy to $33\%$ on medium to $67\%$ on hard, where ten of fifteen solves occurred only after escalation from the 0.5b to the 1.5b model. No solved trial required the 3b model, so escalation contributes capability up to the middle tier, beyond which the remaining failures are capability-saturated or held by the legality stall of Section~\ref{sec:failure-analysis} rather than by a model too small. A direct escalation-off arm on the hard problems (pheromone-field, two agents, 30 trials on the grid seeds, escalation disabled at both the band and model levels) solved $1/30$ against $3/30$ with escalation enabled: the difference is in the predicted direction but not statistically detectable at this sample size (exact McNemar $p = 0.63$), the same power floor that limits the component ablations of Section~\ref{sec:ablations} on instances solved one in ten. We therefore read the escalation evidence as observational, from the model-by-solve composition above, rather than as a controlled causal estimate.

Token efficiency is therefore not where pheromone-field wins; its advantage is coverage. Conversation's solves are confined to easy problems, so its low per-solve cost buys solutions only in the regime where every strategy does reasonably well, whereas pheromone-field's tokens buy solutions at every difficulty, including the medium and hard instances no baseline solves at all.

\textbf{Cost control through escalation.} The escalation mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:escalation}) acts as implicit cost control: trials begin on the smallest model with exploitation sampling, and larger models and wider sampling are recruited only when pressure stalls. Easy problems that solve quickly rarely escalate, so the per-trial cost concentrates on harder instances: pheromone-field averages $345$k tokens per easy trial, $1.0$M per medium, and $1.7$M per hard, the rise tracking the escalation that harder instances trigger.
