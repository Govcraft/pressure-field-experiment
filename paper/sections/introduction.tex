\section{Introduction}

We instantiate Parunak's symmetric-stigmergic schema~\cite{parunak2025insects} in a foundation-model multi-agent system, and characterize the regime in which the resulting decentralized coordination outperforms hierarchical control. Foundation-model multi-agent systems mostly coordinate through explicit dialogue: planners decompose tasks, managers delegate, and workers execute under hierarchical supervision~\cite{wu2023autogen,hong2024metagpt,li2023camel}. That overhead scales poorly. Large-scale studies find that coordination can degrade performance on sequential reasoning tasks~\cite{kim2025scaling}, that most inter-agent messages are prunable~\cite{zhang2025agentprune}, and that gains saturate as agent count grows~\cite{qian2025macnet}.

A second strand suggests these systems coordinate better through the environment than through messages. Language-model agents perform best when coordination rests on environmental state and worst when it requires reasoning about other agents' minds~\cite{agashe2025coordination}, and generative-agent societies organize joint activity through shared co-presence rather than broadcast~\cite{park2023generative}. This is stigmergy, coordination through marks left in a shared environment~\cite{grasse1959stigmergie}. Classic stigmergy is asymmetric, the agent changing the environment but not the reverse; the recent symmetric extension adds the missing arrow, letting the environment also act on agent state, which is exactly what an adaptive multi-agent system built on heterogeneous models needs~\cite{parunak2025insects}.

Coordination operates on a shared \emph{artifact}, a structured object divided into \emph{regions}; in our experiments the artifact is a week's meeting schedule and the regions are its time blocks. Each region's quality deficits are aggregated into a single scalar, its \emph{pressure}, and the \emph{pressure field} is that value over all regions. Agents are foundation-model actors that observe a region's pressure and propose a \emph{patch}, a candidate rewrite of the region's content; coordination emerges from the shared artifact, with no coordinating agent, no planner or manager making content decisions, and no inter-agent message. Two pheromone stores, applied patches kept as few-shot examples and rejected patches kept as warnings, evaporate at each tick of the system's logical clock, following pheromone evaporation~\cite{dorigo1996ant} and the environment-state-dynamics operator~\cite{parunak2011mean}, so stale patches are forgotten rather than left to mislead. Foundation models and stigmergy each supply what the other lacks: foundation models propose the open-ended actions that classic stigmergy could not enumerate, and the pressure criterion combines those proposals and escalates model capability when the field stalls.

This paper makes three contributions. First, it maps a foundation-model artifact-refinement loop onto the symmetric-stigmergic primitives (Section~\ref{sec:symmetric-stigmergy}), including the environment-to-agent escalation arrow that discrete blackboard and co-presence systems leave implicit. Second, it proves that aligned local descent, descent in which lowering a region's pressure also lowers the total, terminates in a number of ticks bounded by initial pressure rather than by state-space size (Section~\ref{sec:theoretical-analysis}). Third, on meeting room scheduling across a regenerated 1350-trial grid, it identifies the regime in which decentralized symmetric-stigmergic coordination outperforms hierarchical control and the smaller-instance regime in which it does not (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
